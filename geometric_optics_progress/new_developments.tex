\section{New Developments: Refraction Map and Extended Refraction Experiments}
\label{sec:new-developments}

\subsection{Derivation of the smooth refraction map}

\subsubsection{Step 1: Write the refractor as an implicit surface}

Starting with the softmin potential,
\begin{align}
 f_\varepsilon(x)
 &=-\varepsilon\log\sum_jq_j
   \exp\!\left(\frac{g_j-c_\kappa(x,y_j)}{\varepsilon}\right),
 \\
 c_\kappa(x,y_j)&=-\log(1-\kappa x\cdot y_j),
\end{align}
the refractor radius is
\begin{equation}
 R(x)=e^{f_\varepsilon(x)}
 =\left[\sum_jq_je^{g_j/\varepsilon}
           (1-\kappa x\cdot y_j)^{1/\varepsilon}\right]^{-\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
Write $a_j=q_je^{g_j/\varepsilon}$. Raising both sides to the power
$-1/\varepsilon$ gives
\[
 R(x)^{-1/\varepsilon}
 =\sum_j a_j(1-\kappa x\cdot y_j)^{1/\varepsilon}.
\]
Multiplying by $R(x)^{1/\varepsilon}$ yields
\[
 \sum_j a_j[R(x)(1-\kappa x\cdot y_j)]^{1/\varepsilon}=1.
\]
Since $p=R(x)x$ and $\|p\|=R(x)$,
\[
 R(x)(1-\kappa x\cdot y_j)=\|p\|-\kappa p\cdot y_j.
\]
Therefore the refractor satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{F(p)=\sum_j a_j(\|p\|-\kappa p\cdot y_j)^{1/\varepsilon}-1=0.}
 \label{eq:new-implicit-refractor}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Step 2: Take the ordinary gradient to obtain a normal}

Using the gradient identities,
\[
 \nabla_p\|p\|=\frac{p}{\|p\|},
 \qquad \nabla_p(\kappa p\cdot y_j)=\kappa y_j.
\]
The chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \nabla F(p)=\frac1\varepsilon\sum_j
 a_j(\|p\|-\kappa p\cdot y_j)^{1/\varepsilon-1}
 \left(\frac{p}{\|p\|}-\kappa y_j\right).
\end{equation}
At the surface point $p=R(x)x$, abbreviate $R=R(x)$ and
$d_j(x)=1-\kappa x\cdot y_j$. Then
\begin{align}
 \nabla F(Rx)
 &=\frac1\varepsilon\sum_j a_j(Rd_j)^{1/\varepsilon-1}
       (x-\kappa y_j) \\
 &=\frac1{\varepsilon R}\sum_j
       a_j(Rd_j)^{1/\varepsilon}\frac{x-\kappa y_j}{d_j}.
 \label{eq:new-gradient-at-radius}
\end{align}
The second line uses
$(Rd_j)^{1/\varepsilon-1}=(Rd_j)^{1/\varepsilon}/(Rd_j)$.
We now explain exactly how its coefficients become the weights $w_j(x)$.

\paragraph{What is $w_j(x)$?}
Define the positive normalizing sum
\[
 Z(x)=\sum_k q_ke^{g_k/\varepsilon}
                  (1-\kappa x\cdot y_k)^{1/\varepsilon}
     =\sum_k a_kd_k(x)^{1/\varepsilon}.
\]
The radius formula says $R(x)=Z(x)^{-\varepsilon}$, so
\[
 R(x)^{1/\varepsilon}=Z(x)^{-1}.
\]
Consequently, each coefficient in
\eqref{eq:new-gradient-at-radius} satisfies
\begin{align}
 a_j[R(x)d_j(x)]^{1/\varepsilon}
 &=a_jR(x)^{1/\varepsilon}d_j(x)^{1/\varepsilon} \\
 &=\frac{a_jd_j(x)^{1/\varepsilon}}{Z(x)} \\
 &=\frac{q_je^{g_j/\varepsilon}
              (1-\kappa x\cdot y_j)^{1/\varepsilon}}
         {\sum_kq_ke^{g_k/\varepsilon}
              (1-\kappa x\cdot y_k)^{1/\varepsilon}} \\
 &=:w_j(x).
 \label{eq:new-softmin-weights}
\end{align}
Equivalently, in the original softmin notation,
\begin{equation}
 w_j(x)=
 \frac{q_j\exp((g_j-c_\kappa(x,y_j))/\varepsilon)}
      {\sum_kq_k\exp((g_k-c_\kappa(x,y_k))/\varepsilon)}.
\end{equation}
Thus $w_j(x)$ is the normalized contribution of target direction $y_j$
to the softmin at $x$: $w_j(x)\geq0$ and $\sum_jw_j(x)=1$.
These local, $x$-dependent weights differ from the prescribed target
masses $q_j$.

Substituting these weights into the gradient gives
\[
 \nabla F(Rx)=\frac1{\varepsilon R}N_\kappa(x),
\]
where the unnormalized normal is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{N_\kappa(x)=\sum_jw_j(x)
              \frac{x-\kappa y_j}{1-\kappa x\cdot y_j}.}
 \label{eq:new-normal}
\end{equation}
Multiplication by $1/(\varepsilon R)>0$ does not change its direction.
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 x\cdot N_\kappa(x)
 =\sum_jw_j(x)\frac{1-\kappa x\cdot y_j}
                       {1-\kappa x\cdot y_j}
 =1.
 \label{eq:new-normal-identity}
\end{equation}
This identity remains $x\cdot N_\kappa=1$; the factors of $\kappa$ cancel
inside the sum. In particular, the normal is nonzero.

\subsubsection{Step 3: Apply the vector formula for refraction}

We can now use the standard vector formula for refraction to find the
push-forward direction $\widehat y(x)$. Let $x$ be the unit incident ray,
$\kappa=n_2/n_1$, and normalize the surface normal:
\[
 n=\frac{N_\kappa(x)}{\|N_\kappa(x)\|},
 \qquad a=x\cdot n=\frac{1}{\|N_\kappa(x)\|}>0.
\]
The incident ray splits into a normal component $an$ and a tangential
component $x-an$. Snell's law divides the tangential component by
$\kappa$. Requiring the refracted ray to have unit length then gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\widehat y(x)=\frac{x-an}{\kappa}
       +\sqrt{1-\frac{1-a^2}{\kappa^2}}\,n.}
 \label{eq:new-snell-map}
\end{equation}
We choose the positive square root so that the transmitted ray points
through the surface in the direction of $n$. We also check
\[
 D(x)=1-\frac{1-a^2}{\kappa^2}\geq0.
\]
A negative value means total internal reflection: there is no real
transmitted direction. The computation flags this case as an error when
checking the interpolation, rather than dropping the ray. The resulting
push-forward measure is $\alpha_{\mathrm{ent}}=\sum_i p_i
\delta_{\widehat y(x_i)}$.

\subsection{Extended refraction experiments: common setup}

We compare the hard $c$-transform map with the smooth surface and Snell
map derived above. The source and target use the earlier Halton patches,
with uniform discrete weights
\[
 p_i=\frac1{N_K},\qquad q_j=\frac1{1600},\qquad\kappa=0.6.
\]
The target stays fixed at 1600 points. We use 40 logarithmically spaced
source counts from 100 to 20,000, rounded to integers, and 40 refractor
regularizations from $6.25\times10^{-5}$ to $0.025$:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Vary $N_K$ and set
 $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{refr}}=1/(8\lfloor\sqrt{N_K}\rfloor)$.
 \item Vary $N_K$ with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{refr}}=1/320$.
 \item Fix $N_K=1600$ and vary $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{refr}}$.
\end{enumerate}
The optical transport and self solves allow up to 2000 final updates,
with potential-change tolerance $10^{-5}$. Small-grid warm starts and
continuation are retained. The independent transport evaluations use
$\eta=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eval}}=1/320$ and tolerance $10^{-9}$.
Thus changing the refractor epsilon does not change the evaluation metric.

The results captured on October 26, 2026 at 11:00 PM ET contain all 120 hard-map cases and
120 entropic-map cases: 40 in experiment 1, 40 in
experiment 2, and 40 in experiment 3. Both maps now cover the full grid.

\subsection{Push-forward maps and the evaluation cost}

For the hard map, each source ray is assigned to a target direction:
\[
 j^*(i)=\underset{j}{\operatorname{argmin}}
 \{c_\kappa(x_i,y_j)-g_j^{\mathrm{raw}}\},\qquad
 z_i^{\mathrm{hard}}=y_{j^*(i)}.
\]
For the entropic map, $z_i^{\mathrm{ent}}=\widehat y(x_i)$ from
\eqref{eq:new-snell-map}. These directions need not be target sample
points. In either case, write
\[
 \alpha=\sum_i p_i\delta_{z_i},\qquad
 \nu=\sum_j q_j\delta_{y_j}.
\]
We first evaluate the entropically regularized transport cost
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{EOT}_\eta(\alpha,\nu)
 =\min_{\pi\in\Pi(p,q)}\left\{
 \sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\|z_i-y_j\|_2^2
 +\eta\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\log\frac{\pi_{ij}}{p_iq_j}\right\},
 \label{eq:new-eot-evaluation}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi(p,q)$ consists of nonnegative matrices with row sums $p_i$
and column sums $q_j$. The first term measures squared spatial
separation in three dimensions; the second penalizes departure from the
product coupling $p\otimes q$.

Even when the two measures agree, this regularized cost can be positive.
We therefore also compute the debiased Sinkhorn divergence:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{S_\eta(\alpha,\nu)=\operatorname{EOT}_\eta(\alpha,\nu)
 -\tfrac12\operatorname{EOT}_\eta(\alpha,\alpha)
 -\tfrac12\operatorname{EOT}_\eta(\nu,\nu).}
 \label{eq:new-divergence}
\end{equation}
The self terms remove each measure's regularization bias. The plots show
$S_\eta$ itself, without taking a square root. This is a regularized
comparison with squared Euclidean ground cost, rather than the exact
unregularized Wasserstein distance.

\subsection{Results of the three experiments}

The hard map has lower Sinkhorn divergence in 120 of the 120
matched cases. All three EOT terms passed the evaluation stopping test
for every published divergence. Figure~\ref{fig:new-divergence} shows
the latest comparison.

\paragraph{Experiment 1: Joint movement.}
As $N_K$ increases from 100 to 20,000 and the refractor epsilon
decreases, the hard-map divergence falls from
$0.000977$ to $1.43\times10^{-5}$. The entropic-map divergence
falls from $0.00113$ to $2.01\times10^{-5}$.
Both improve substantially, although changing two parameters together
does not isolate their effects.

\paragraph{Experiment 2: Fixed refractor epsilon.}
Increasing the source count alone gives little improvement after the
initial changes. The hard-map divergence ranges from
$0.000121$ to $0.000142$; the entropic map stays
between $0.000155$ and $0.000158$.
Refining the source at this fixed epsilon and target does not remove
the observed error floor.

\paragraph{Experiment 3: Fixed source count.}
For the hard map, divergence increases from
$9.14\times10^{-8}$ to $0.00213$ across the full epsilon grid.
The 40 completed entropic cases cover
$6.25\times10^{-5}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{refr}}\leq 0.025$.
Their endpoint divergences are $1.92\times10^{-7}$ and $0.0025$.

Raw EOT can give a different ranking. At the final point of experiment 1
it is $0.0060977$ for the hard map and $0.0058235$ for the entropic map.
After self-term subtraction, the hard map has the smaller divergence.
A lower cross-EOT value alone does not establish a closer target match.

\subsection{Mass errors in the hard push-forward}

Several source rays can map to the same target direction. Let
\[
 m_j=\#\{i:j^*(i)=j\},\qquad
 \alpha(\{y_j\})=\frac{m_j}{N_K}.
\]
The maximum absolute target-mass error is
\begin{equation}
 E_\infty=\max_{1\leq j\leq1600}
 \left|\frac{m_j}{N_K}-\frac1{1600}\right|.
\end{equation}
Empty target locations have $m_j=0$ and are included. This measures a
probability-mass discrepancy at individual target points; it does not
account for the distance between those points.

In experiment 1, $E_\infty$ decreases overall from $0.059375$ to
$0.005575$, with intermediate fluctuations. In experiment 2 it changes
from $0.019375$ to $0.018125$. In experiment 3 it increases from
$0.00125$ to $0.109375$ as the refractor epsilon increases.

We also count target locations whose mass differs from one source weight:
\[
 C=\#\{j:m_j/N_K\ne1/N_K\}
  =\#\{j:m_j=0\}+\#\{j:m_j\geq2\}.
\]
This counts empty locations plus locations receiving multiple source
rays. Its reference is $1/N_K$, whereas the prescribed target mass is
$1/1600$. For example, at $N_K=20000$ a perfectly matched target would
require $m_j=12.5$, which is impossible for integer multiplicities.
Thus $C$ is an occupancy diagnostic, not a count of incorrect target
masses. In experiment 1 the empty-location count falls from 1535 to zero,
while $C$ changes from 1552 to 1597.

\subsection{Stopping conditions and interpretation}

For the optical plan
\[
 \gamma_{ij}=p_iq_j\exp\!\left(
 \frac{f_i^{\mathrm{raw}}+g_j^{\mathrm{raw}}-c_\kappa(x_i,y_j)}
 {\varepsilon_{\mathrm{refr}}}\right),
\]
we check the source and target marginal residuals
\[
 r_s=\sum_i\left|\sum_j\gamma_{ij}-p_i\right|,\qquad
 r_t=\sum_j\left|\sum_i\gamma_{ij}-q_j\right|.
\]
In the earlier 17-update hard-map run, their maximum values were
$0.17678$ and $0.54792$. Raising both final-loop caps to 2000 reduces
both maxima to approximately $0.014721$. All 120 hard-map transport
solves and their self solves met the $10^{-5}$ potential-change tolerance
before reaching these caps. The longest final loops used 659 transport,
551 source-self, and 15 target-self updates.

The completed entropic run also has maximum source and target residuals of
approximately $0.014721$. Across its 120 saved maps,
$|x\cdot N_\kappa-1|<9.1\times10^{-13}$ and
$|\|\widehat y\|-1|<7.8\times10^{-16}$ at all sampled rays.

Small potential changes do not guarantee exact marginal feasibility.
The optical marginal residuals, convergence of the independent EOT
calculations, and agreement of the pushed measure with the target are
separate checks. The reported trends describe the tested grids and
completed cases; they do not establish an asymptotic convergence rate.
